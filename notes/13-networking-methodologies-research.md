## 1. The Academic Foundation: What the Evidence Actually Says

### 1a. Mark Granovetter — "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973, Stanford)

**The finding:** In a survey of 282 men in Boston, Granovetter found that people found jobs through casual acquaintances (weak ties) more often than through close friends (strong ties). Weak ties bridge you to networks outside your own circle, giving you information you wouldn't otherwise access.

**The 2022 MIT/Stanford mega-experiment (Rajkumar et al., Science):** 20 million LinkedIn users, 5-year experiment, 2 billion connections, 600,000 jobs. Results:
- **Weak ties are confirmed to produce more job mobility than strong ties.** The largest causal test ever conducted.
- **Nuance: It's an upside-down U shape.** "Moderately weak ties" (around 10 mutual connections) are the sweet spot. Very weak ties and very strong ties both underperform.
- **Industry matters:** Weak ties are even MORE important in digital/tech industries. In analog industries, stronger ties can matter more.
- **Source:** Rajkumar, Saint-Jacques, Bojinov, Brynjolfsson, Aral (2022). "A Causal Test of the Strength of Weak Ties." *Science*. Cited 340+ times.

**What this means for Rui:** His 600+ FARFETCH alumni are PERFECT weak-to-moderate ties. They share enough context (FARFETCH) to have mutual connections, but have scattered into new companies and networks. They are exactly the "moderately weak ties" the research says are highest-yield. He doesn't need to meet strangers — he needs to reactivate people who already know and respect him.

### 1b. Daniel Z. Levin — "Dormant Ties: The Value of Reconnecting" (2011, Organization Science)

**The finding:** Levin, Walter, and Murnighan (Rutgers/GWU/Northwestern Kellogg) introduced the concept of "dormant ties" — relationships that have gone inactive. In a study of Executive MBA students, they found:
- **Reconnecting dormant ties produced outcomes comparable to active ties** for work projects.
- **Reconnecting previously STRONG dormant ties gave BOTH weak-tie benefits (efficiency, novelty) AND strong-tie benefits (trust, shared perspective).** This is the best of both worlds — you get the trust of an old friend plus the new information of someone who's been learning new things during the dormancy period.
- **Dormant ties are underutilized** — most people don't think to reconnect.
- **Cited 421+ times.** Source: Organization Science, Vol. 22, No. 4.

**The 2023 MIT Sloan Management Review update (Rondi, Levin, De Massis):** Identified THREE key elements for successful dormant tie reconnection:
1. **How well both parties remember each other** (the relationship needs shared history)
2. **How they go about catching up** (there's a wrong way — see pitfalls below)
3. **Whether they perceive the relationship similarly** (both need to remember it as positive)

**The COVID-era study (Yang et al., 2021, J. Applied Psychology):** People experiencing job insecurity were MORE likely to reactivate dormant ties — and it functioned as a coping mechanism. The reactivation was greater among people under stress. **Rui's situation (16 months unemployed, financial pressure) is exactly the trigger the research describes.**

**What this means for Rui:** This is the single most important research finding for his situation. He has 600+ dormant ties from FARFETCH — people who respected him, worked with him, and have spent the last 2+ years building new networks and learning new things. Reconnecting with them gives him:
- Trust already established (no cold outreach needed)
- Novel information (they've moved to new companies, learned new things)
- Zero risk of rejection from a stranger (they already know him)

### 1c. Adam Grant — "Give and Take" (2013, Wharton)

**The finding:** Grant identified three reciprocity styles:
- **Takers:** extract value, self-serving
- **Matchers:** quid pro quo, transactional
- **Givers:** generously help others without expecting return

**Key finding for Rui:** The most SUCCESSFUL long-term networkers are givers — but specifically "otherish givers" (givers who also protect their own interests), not "selfless givers" (who burn out). Givers build deeper, more loyal networks because people remember who helped them.

**The critical nuance:** Grant's research shows givers are ALSO the LEAST successful when they're selfless (they get exploited). The winning profile is "otherish giving" — help others, but set boundaries.

**Adam Rifkin's "5-Minute Favor"** (Fortune's "Best Networker in Silicon Valley" 2011, endorsed by Grant): The methodology is to do small favors that cost you 5 minutes but create high value for the other person — e.g., introducing two people who should know each other, sharing a useful article, recommending someone for a role. Rifkin was LinkedIn's most-connected person to Fortune 500 execs, built entirely through small acts of generosity.

**What this means for Rui:** As a Relator, Rui already operates this way — he invests deeply in people he cares about. The "5-Minute Favor" reframes networking from "asking for things" to "offering small help." For a Relator/High-C, offering help is far less aversive than asking for it. The methodology: don't ask "can you help me find a job?" — instead, share an article relevant to their work, offer to make an introduction, or give feedback on something they're building.

### 1d. Daniel McFarland — Social Network Formation (Stanford GSE)

McFarland's research on how social networks form in organizational settings finds that networks emerge from **repeated, structured interactions around shared tasks** — not from social events. People who work on cross-functional projects develop ties that persist. His "Network Ecology" work (2014, cited 371+ times) shows that the *environment* shapes network formation more than individual personality traits.

**What this means for Rui:** His FARFETCH network was built through exactly this mechanism — 15 years of shared work, cross-functional projects, vendor relationships. These ties are real and durable. He doesn't need to "network" — he needs to *reactivate* work-based relationships that already exist. McFarland's research validates that the relationships formed through shared work are the strongest, and they persist even after long dormancy.

The evidence points to a clear hierarchy of yield for senior executive job search:

| Channel | Yield | Evidence |

**The implication:** Rui's highest-yield activity is NOT cold applications or cold LinkedIn outreach. It is **reactivating dormant ties who can refer him or introduce him to hiring managers**. ## 3. Five Candidate Methodologies

### Methodology #1: The TIARA Informational Interview Framework

**Source:** Steve Dalton, Senior Career Consultant at Duke University's Fuqua School of Business. Book: *The 2-Hour Job Search* (2012, 2nd ed. 2020, Ten Speed Press / Random House).

**Evidence base:** Dalton's methodology is built on the premise that hiring managers increasingly rely on referrals to avoid reviewing hundreds of resumes. The TIARA framework structures the informational interview to maximize relationship-building and minimize the "what do I say?" anxiety. It's used by MBA programs at Duke, BYU, Nebraska, and Notre Dame, among others. The "2-Hour" framing is deliberate — it breaks the job search into manageable 15-minute chunks, which is specifically designed for people who find the process overwhelming.

**The framework — TIARA stands for:**
- **T**rends: What's changing in the field/industry? What skills are becoming essential?
- **I**nsights: Why did this work interest you? What are the satisfactions and challenges?
- **A**dvice: How did you get your job? What's the best way to enter this field?
- **R**esources: What should I read? Who else should I talk to? (This is where referrals happen naturally)
- **A**ssignments: What experiences or training should I pursue?

**Specific steps:**
1. Identify a person in a role/company/industry you want to learn about
2. Request a 20-30 minute conversation (NOT a job ask — explicitly "I'm researching [field] and would value your perspective")
3. Prepare 5-7 TIARA questions in advance (from the framework above)
4. Conduct the conversation: listen 80%, talk 20%. Ask for resources/referrals at the end
5. Follow up within 24 hours with a thank-you that references something specific they said
6. If they referred you to someone, contact that person using their name ("X suggested I reach out")

**The critical rule:** NEVER ask for a job in an informational interview. You ask for information, ideas, and referrals. The job ask happens later, if at all — the relationship does the work.

**Fit assessment for Rui:**
✅ **Strong fit.** TIARA provides a SCRIPT — which is exactly what a High-C/Analytical profile needs. The structure removes the "what do I say?" anxiety.
✅ **Depth over breadth.** Each conversation is a deep 1-on-1, not a room-spray. Relator-compatible.
✅ **Research framing.** It's an *interview*, not *networking*. Rui can frame it as intelligence gathering, which his Learner #3 strength will enjoy.
⚠️ **Moderate barrier.** He still has to initiate the conversation, which is the hard part. But the script helps.
✅ **Low volume.** 2-3 conversations per week, not 100 messages.

**Verdict: Recommend as the STRUCTURE for conversations once contact is made. Pair with Methodology #2 for the reactivation step.**

### Methodology #2: Dormant Tie Reactivation Protocol ⭐ TOP RECOMMENDATION

**Source:** Academic research by Levin, Walter & Murnighan (2011, Organization Science, cited 421+); Rondi, Levin & De Massis (2023, MIT Sloan Management Review); Yang et al. (2021, J. Applied Psychology). Synthesized into a practical protocol.

**Evidence base:** This is the strongest evidence-based methodology for Rui's specific situation. The research shows:
- Reconnecting dormant ties produces outcomes comparable to active ties (Levin et al., 2011)
- Previously STRONG dormant ties give BOTH weak-tie benefits (novelty, new networks) AND strong-tie benefits (trust, shared perspective) — a unique "best of both worlds"
- People under job insecurity are naturally drawn to reactivate dormant ties (Yang et al., 2021) — this is a normal, researched coping mechanism, not a sign of desperation
- The MIT/Stanford LinkedIn experiment (2022) confirms that moderately weak ties (people you share ~10 mutual connections with) are the highest-yield for job mobility — this perfectly describes former colleagues from the same company

**The three elements of successful reactivation (Rondi et al., 2023):**
1. **Shared memory** — both parties must remember the relationship positively (Rui's former team members and colleagues do)
2. **Proper catch-up** — the reactivation message needs to acknowledge the gap and catch up before making any ask
3. **Symmetric perception** — both parties need to view the relationship similarly (checking: did this person see Rui as a boss/colleague they respected?)

**The Dormant Tie Reactivation Protocol — Specific Steps:**

**Step 1: Map and prioritize (see Section 4 for the full mapping system)**
- List dormant contacts from FARFETCH (600+ people)
- Tier them: Tier A = former direct reports + close cross-functional partners (highest trust); Tier B = extended team + vendors + agency partners; Tier C = industry contacts
- Start with Tier A — these are the people most likely to remember the relationship warmly and respond

**Step 2: The reactivation message (use this exact structure)**
The research says the reactivation message must do three things: (a) acknowledge the gap, (b) catch up genuinely, (c) make a LOW-stakes ask. Here's the template:

> "Hi [Name], it's been a while — I think we last connected at [specific shared context: project/event/when they left FARFETCH]. I hope you're doing well. I saw [specific recent thing about them — LinkedIn update, company news, etc.] and wanted to reach out. I'm currently exploring [my situation framed as exploration, not desperation — e.g., "looking at senior creative ops roles in [sector]"] and would really value your perspective on [specific thing they'd know about — their company, their sector, what they're seeing in the market]. Would you have 20 minutes for a quick call sometime in the next couple weeks? No pressure at all — I'd just love to catch up and hear what you're working on."

**Critical principles from the research:**
- **Acknowledge the dormancy explicitly** — pretending no time has passed feels manipulative (Rondi et al., 2023)
- **Catch up FIRST, ask SECOND** — the most common reactivation mistake is leading with the ask
- **Make a LOW-stakes ask** — a 20-minute chat, not "can you get me a job?"
- **Reference something specific and recent** about them — this proves you're not mass-messaging
- **Frame YOUR situation as exploration** — "I'm exploring/researching/looking at" not "I'm desperate/unemployed"

**Step 3: The conversation (use TIARA framework from Methodology #1)**
- Once they say yes, use the TIARA question structure
- Listen 80%, talk 20%
- Ask "Who else should I talk to?" at the end (the referral ask — this is where the network expands naturally)
- DO NOT ask them for a job directly. If they know of something, they'll mention it.

**Step 4: The follow-up**
- Within 24 hours: thank-you note referencing something specific they said
- If they referred you: contact the referral, name-drop ("[Name] suggested I reach out")
- Add to relationship tracker with date, topic, and next-step reminder
- Follow up again in 4-6 weeks with something relevant (an article, a connection, an update)

**Step 5: Cadence**
- **2-3 reactivation messages per week** (not 50 — this is low-volume, depth-focused)
- Start with Tier A (5-10 people), do NOT proceed to Tier B until Tier A is exhausted
- After each conversation, log the outcome. Patterns will emerge about which sectors/contacts are yielding leads.

**Fit assessment for Rui:**
✅ **EXCELLENT fit.** This is the methodology MOST aligned with Rui's profile:
- Relator #1: These are people he already has a relationship with — he can be genuine, not performative
- Deliberative #2: The structured protocol (map → tier → template → TIARA → follow-up) gives him a system, not an open-ended anxiety
- Learner #3: Framing conversations as "research" and "learning about the market" is exactly how his Learner strength would engage
- Analytical #4: He can track outcomes, measure response rates, analyze which tiers/sectors yield best
- High-C: The script-based approach reduces the unstructured social anxiety
✅ **Reframes networking as reconnecting.** "Catching up with former colleagues" is categorically different from "networking" — it's less aversive and the research says it's higher-yield.
✅ **Low volume.** 2-3 per week, not 100.
✅ **Depth over breadth.** Each conversation is a real conversation with someone who knows him.
✅ **Highest evidence base.** Three separate peer-reviewed studies support this specific approach.
✅ **Uses the 600+ FARFETCH network he already has.** He doesn't need to build a network — he needs to activate one that already exists.
⚠️ **Minor risk: some contacts may have moved on.** Not everyone will respond. The research says dormant tie reactivation has a good response rate, but it's not 100%. Rui should expect 30-50% response rate from Tier A.

**Verdict: ⭐ TOP RECOMMENDATION. This is the methodology most aligned with Rui's profile, the strongest evidence base, and the most directly applicable to his specific situation (large dormant network from one company).**

### Methodology #3: The 5-Minute Favor / "Give-First" Framework

**Source:** Adam Rifkin (Fortune's "Best Networker" 2011) + Adam Grant's "Give and Take" research (Wharton, 2013). Rifkin was LinkedIn's most-connected person to Fortune 500 execs — built entirely through small acts of generosity, not by asking for anything.

**Evidence base:** Grant's research at Wharton showed that "otherish givers" (people who consistently help others with small favors while protecting their own interests) are the most successful long-term networkers. They build deeper, more loyal networks than takers or matchers. Rifkin's 5-Minute Favor concept is Grant's favorite networking idea — it's been featured in Forbes, Big Think, and Grant's own work.

**The principle:** Do small favors that cost you 5 minutes but create high value for the other person. Never lead with an ask. The reciprocity builds naturally over time — people who've been helped by you will think of you when opportunities arise.

**Specific steps:**
1. Identify someone in your network you haven't spoken to recently
2. Find something you can do for them in 5 minutes:
   - Introduce them to someone they should know
   - Share an article relevant to their work
   - Recommend them for a role or opportunity
   - Give feedback on something they've built/written
   - Endorse them on LinkedIn for a skill you've directly observed
   - Share their content with your network
3. Send it with NO ask attached: "Saw this and thought of you — no response needed"
4. Do this for 2-3 people per week
5. After 3-4 exchanges, it becomes natural to ask: "I'm exploring [thing] — would you have 20 minutes to chat?"

**Fit assessment for Rui:**
✅ **Strong fit for the GIVING part.** As a Relator, Rui naturally invests in people he cares about. Offering help is far less aversive than asking for it.
✅ **Low volume.** 2-3 favors per week, 5 minutes each.
✅ **No cold outreach.** This works with existing/dormant contacts, not strangers.
✅ **Reframes networking as generosity.** For a High-C/Relator, "helping people" is a comfortable frame.
⚠️ **Asymmetric.** The giving builds social capital, but it's a slow burn — it doesn't generate immediate leads. In Rui's urgent situation (16 months unemployed), this is a SUPPORTING methodology, not a primary one.
⚠️ **The "ask" still has to happen eventually.** Rui will eventually need to ask for help, which is the core aversion. This methodology delays but doesn't eliminate the ask.

**Verdict: Good as a SUPPORTING methodology alongside #2. The "5-Minute Favor" is the perfect way to warm up a dormant tie BEFORE the reactivation ask. Send a favor first, then a week later, send the reactivation message. This two-step sequence (give → then reconnect) is ideal for a Relator.**

### Methodology #4: The Introvert's Monthly Networking Routine (2 Hours/Week)

**Source:** Synthesized from introvert networking research including Wharton relationship-quality studies (2024), LinkedIn Economic Graph data (2025), and the introvert networking framework articulated by Orvo and others. Adam Grant's research on giver-style networking is foundational.

**Evidence base:**
- Wharton study (2024): Professionals who maintained 15-25 deep connections generated 2.4x more career opportunities than those with 100+ shallow connections. **Depth beats breadth.**
- LinkedIn Economic Graph (2025): Professionals who maintain consistent monthly touchpoints with their top 20 contacts are 4.5x more likely to receive unsolicited career opportunities than those who only reach out when they need something.
- The research consensus: introverts build STRONGER networks than extroverts when they play to their strengths (depth, preparation, listening, written follow-up) rather than imitating extrovert tactics (room-working, volume, spontaneity).

**The 2-Hour Weekly Routine:**

**Week 1: Maintenance (30 min)**
- Review your contact list: who is overdue for a follow-up?
- Send 2-3 personalized messages to existing contacts (share an article, congratulate a promotion, ask about a project they mentioned)
- Update notes on recent conversations

**Week 2: One Deep Conversation (60 min)**
- One 1-on-1 meeting (coffee, video call) with someone in your network
- Prepare in advance: review what you know about them, prepare 3 thoughtful questions (TIARA)
- Log the conversation immediately after and set next follow-up date

**Week 3: Content Contribution (30 min)**
- Write one thoughtful LinkedIn comment on a post by someone in your network
- OR share one insight from your work that could help others
- This builds visibility without real-time social interaction

**Week 4: Strategic Outreach (30 min)**
- Identify 1-2 new people to connect with (or dormant ties to reactivate)
- Send a personalized connection request referencing something specific about their work
- Add to your tracker with initial context

**Monthly total: ~8 hours of intentional networking — zero cocktail hours.**

**Fit assessment for Rui:**
✅ **Strong fit for the cadence.** 2 hours/week, structured, low volume. A Deliberative/High-C profile can work a system like this.
✅ **Depth over breadth.** 1 deep conversation per week, not 20 surface interactions.
✅ **Written over verbal.** The content contribution week leverages written communication, which introverts prefer.
✅ **Preparation over spontaneity.** Every interaction is prepared, not improvised.
⚠️ **Too slow for urgency.** Rui has been unemployed 16 months. A 6-month compounding routine may be too slow — he needs to front-load the reactivation (Methodology #2) first, then shift to this maintenance routine.
⚠️ **Content contribution may feel performative.** Writing LinkedIn comments/posts may trigger the High-C aversion to self-promotion. Make this optional.

**Verdict: Good as a MAINTENANCE routine AFTER the initial dormant tie reactivation campaign (Methodology #2) is underway. Don't start here — start with #2, then transition to this rhythm after 4-6 weeks.**

## 4. Network Mapping for a 600+ Contact Dormant Network

This is the specific, practical system for how Rui maps his FARFETCH network without becoming overwhelmed. It uses a tier-and-segment approach borrowed from executive talent mapping methodology (Cornerstone Search, 2026) and adapted for an individual's dormant network.

### The problem
Rui has 600+ contacts from 15 years at FARFETCH — former direct reports, extended team members, cross-functional colleagues, vendors, agency partners, industry contacts. Mapping all 600 at once is a Deliberative/High-C nightmare: it's overwhelming, the first step to paralysis. The key is to NOT map all 600.

### The Tier-and-Segment System

**Step 1: Export and segment (1 hour, one time)**
Export LinkedIn connections (Settings → Data Privacy → Get a copy of your data → Connections). This gives a CSV with first name, last name, company, position, and date connected.

Segment the CSV into 5 buckets:
1. **Former direct reports** (people who reported to Rui directly or through his direct reports)
2. **Cross-functional peers/leaders** (people at similar level in other functions — tech, product, marketing, finance, HR, operations)
3. **Vendors and agency partners** (external service providers who worked with his team)
4. **Industry contacts** (people met at conferences, industry events, through FARFETCH business)
5. **Other** (everyone else)

**Step 2: Tier each segment (focus, don't boil the ocean)**
Within each segment, tier by relationship strength:
- **Tier A:** People who would definitely take your call. Former direct reports you had a close working relationship with. Cross-functional peers you partnered on major projects. Vendors you worked with for years. (~30-50 people total)
- **Tier B:** People who would probably respond. Extended team members, people you collaborated with but weren't close to. (~100-150 people)
- **Tier C:** People who might respond. Industry contacts, people you met once or twice. (~200-300 people)
- **Tier D:** Everyone else. Don't map these yet. (~200+ people)

**Only Tier A and Tier B go on the active list. That's ~130-200 people — still a lot, but manageable at 2-3 per week.**

**Step 3: Add warm contacts already identified**
- André Eiras (Sword Health, met at Porto Business School) — Tier A, warm
- Michelle Rowe (former direct report, now at Fashion Nova) — Tier A, warm

**Step 4: Create the Market Intelligence Tracker (not a "networking tracker")**
Use a simple spreadsheet (or Notion/Obsidian). Columns:
| Name | Company now | Sector | Relationship (direct report/peer/vendor) | Tier (A/B/C) | Last contacted | Status | Next action | Notes |
|-----|------------|--------|------------------------------------------|-------------|---------------|--------|-------------|-------|

**Status values:** Not contacted → Message sent → Conversation scheduled → Conversation done → Referral received → Lead identified → Closed

**Step 5: Start with 5 people from Tier A**
Do NOT try to contact all 50 Tier A people at once. Start with 5. Use the Dormant Tie Reactivation Protocol. See what happens. Then do 5 more. The Deliberative strength needs to test the system on a small batch before committing to the full list.

### Why this mapping system works for Rui's profile
- **Deliberative #2:** The tier system means he's not "boiling the ocean" — he's starting with the highest-trust contacts and expanding only as the system proves itself. Risk is contained.
- **Analytical #4:** The tracker is a data system. He can see response rates, which sectors are yielding leads, which tiers convert best.
- **High-C:** The process is systematic and documented. Nothing is left to improvisation.
- **Relator #1:** Tier A is people he already has a real relationship with — the starting point is deep, not shallow.
- **Learner #3:** Each conversation adds to the "Market Intelligence" document — the knowledge base grows with every interaction.
